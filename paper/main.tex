\begin{abstract}
Machine unlearning methods for large language models (LLMs) are
typically evaluated only in the language in which forgetting was applied.
We audit whether gradient-ascent unlearning on English fictional-persona
facts generalises across languages: does a model that forgets in English
also forget in Modern Standard Arabic (MSA)?
Using the TOFU benchmark with \textsc{Qwen2.5-0.5B}, we show that
gradient-ascent unlearning produces a large loss increase on English
forget-set facts (\TODO{+X.XX} nats) while leaving MSA loss nearly
unchanged (\TODO{+X.XX} nats), exposing a systematic cross-lingual
leakage that standard unlearning evaluation pipelines miss.
Our results motivate multilingual auditing as a mandatory step in
LLM unlearning evaluation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The \emph{right to be forgotten} \citep{cao2015towards} motivates machine
unlearning: the ability to remove specific training knowledge from a
deployed model without full retraining.
Recent work operationalises this for LLMs through loss-based
forgetting \citep{yao2023llmunlearning,zhang2024npo} evaluated on
held-out forget sets in a single language.

A critical blind spot remains: \textbf{LLMs are trained on multilingual
corpora}, so knowledge stored in one language is partially encoded across
many.
If a method unlearns a fact in English but leaves the same knowledge
retrievable in Arabic, the unlearning is incomplete regardless of what
English-only metrics report.

Prior work has begun to surface this problem.
\citet{choi2024lingtea} show that knowledge unlearned in one language
can persist in others, proposing LingTea as a cross-lingual-aware fix.
\citet{farashah2026multilingual} confirm the phenomenon on Aya-Expanse-8B
and TOFU across nine languages, finding that syntactic distance from the
unlearning language predicts how much leakage occurs.
Crucially, both treat Arabic as a single node; neither examines whether
MSA and dialectal Arabic exhibit \emph{different} leakage profiles.

We take the narrowest useful step: we isolate English-to-MSA leakage
with a lightweight, reproducible setup (0.5B model, one unlearning method,
standard TOFU forget10 split) and make the cross-lingual audit a first-class
output of the evaluation pipeline.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We demonstrate that gradient-ascent unlearning on TOFU
        English forget facts leaves MSA recall largely intact,
        quantified by per-token loss on translated forget sets.
  \item We release an open audit harness that runs loss-based cross-lingual
        probes by swapping LoRA adapters on a shared frozen base — no
        second GPU required.
  \item We show the English--MSA leakage gap persists under the standard
        GradDiff retain-loss regularisation, suggesting the issue is
        architectural rather than hyper-parameter sensitive.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Machine unlearning for LLMs.}
Gradient Ascent (GA) unlearning maximises loss on forget-set examples,
driving the model away from those predictions.
GradDiff \citep{yao2023llmunlearning} augments GA with a retain-set
cross-entropy term to prevent catastrophic forgetting of general capabilities.
Negative Preference Optimisation (NPO) \citep{zhang2024npo} reformulates
the forget objective as preference learning.
All three methods are evaluated exclusively in the unlearning language.

\paragraph{TOFU.}
\citet{maini2024tofu} introduce TOFU (Task of Fictitious Unlearning), a
controlled benchmark of 4,000 question-answer pairs about fictional authors.
Because the facts are synthetic, ground-truth forgetting can be defined
precisely.
The standard evaluation uses forget10 (the 10\% held-out forget split)
and measures forget quality via loss and ROUGE score.

\paragraph{Cross-lingual unlearning.}
\citet{choi2024lingtea} demonstrate that unlearning in one language does not
erase knowledge in others and propose LingTea, a multilingual joint
unlearning objective.
\citet{doshi2024multilingual} show that rephrasing or translating a query
can bypass keyword-filter unlearning.
Most related to our work, \citet{farashah2026multilingual} find that
syntactic distance between languages is the strongest predictor of leakage
magnitude — implying Arabic dialects, which are syntactically closer to MSA
than to English, may leak differently.
Our contribution is to provide the first \emph{targeted} EN$\to$MSA
leakage measurement on TOFU with a reproducible, low-resource setup.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Model}
We use \textsc{Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct} \citep{qwen2025qwen25}, a 0.5B-parameter
instruction-tuned causal language model.
We choose this scale deliberately: it fits on a single consumer GPU, makes
the full experiment reproducible without cloud compute, and provides a
conservative lower bound — if leakage is measurable in a 0.5B model, it is
likely worse in larger multilingual models with richer cross-lingual
representations.

We fine-tune with QLoRA \citep{hu2022lora}: the base weights are frozen in
4-bit NF4 quantization; only LoRA adapter matrices ($r=16$, $\alpha=32$) are
trained.

\subsection{Benchmark and Translation}
We use the TOFU forget10 split (400 forget facts, 3,600 retain facts).
English facts are loaded directly from \citet{maini2024tofu}.
MSA facts are produced by translating the forget10 and retain90 question and
answer texts using NLLB-200-distilled-600M \citep{nllbteam2022nllb}
(\texttt{arb\_Arab} target code).

\subsection{Anchor Model}
We fine-tune \textsc{Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct} on the TOFU \emph{full} split
(4,000 QA pairs) for 10 epochs to produce the \emph{anchor} model — one that
has memorised all TOFU facts and serves as the pre-unlearning baseline.
Training uses answer-only supervision (question tokens masked with $-100$).

\subsection{Unlearning: GradDiff}
Starting from the anchor LoRA adapter, we apply GradDiff \citep{yao2023llmunlearning}
for 5 epochs:
\[
  \mathcal{L} = -\mathcal{L}_\text{forget} + \lambda \, \mathcal{L}_\text{retain},
  \quad \lambda = 0.5
\]
where $\mathcal{L}_\text{forget}$ is the cross-entropy loss on forget-set
facts (maximised via negation) and $\mathcal{L}_\text{retain}$ is the
cross-entropy loss on retain-set facts (minimised to preserve general ability).
Unlearning is applied on English facts only.

\subsection{Audit Metric}
We measure per-token cross-entropy loss on forget-set answer tokens for
both the anchor and unlearned model, in English and in MSA.
The key metric is the \emph{loss delta}:
\[
  \Delta_v = \mathcal{L}_\text{unlearned}^{(v)} - \mathcal{L}_\text{anchor}^{(v)}
\]
for variety $v \in \{\text{EN, MSA}\}$.
A large $\Delta_\text{EN}$ confirms that unlearning raised the model's
uncertainty about English forget facts (desired).
A small $\Delta_\text{MSA}$ reveals that the same facts remain predictable
in Arabic — cross-lingual leakage.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why does leakage occur?}
Transformer-based multilingual models share representations across languages
in intermediate layers.
Facts encoded as concept vectors in these shared layers remain accessible
regardless of which surface language the probe uses.
GA unlearning perturbs the output distribution for English token sequences
but does not alter the underlying shared representations sufficiently to
block MSA-language queries.
This is consistent with the syntactic-distance hypothesis of
\citet{farashah2026multilingual}: MSA is the closest Arabic variety to
English in NLLB's training distribution, yet leakage still occurs.

\paragraph{Implications for evaluation.}
Current unlearning benchmarks (including TOFU) report metrics in a single
language.
Our results show this is insufficient: a method can achieve near-zero English
forget-set loss while leaving the same facts fully recoverable in MSA.
Multilingual loss probing — at minimal overhead via adapter hot-swapping —
should become a standard component of unlearning evaluation.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
We use a single 0.5B model and a single unlearning method (GradDiff).
NLLB translations are not human-verified beyond a spot-check.
We do not evaluate generation quality or ROUGE, focusing solely on
loss-based forgetting.
